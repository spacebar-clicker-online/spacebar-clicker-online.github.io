How to press the spacebar faster

Speed on the spacebar comes from moving less, not trying harder. These are the changes that make the biggest difference, in the order to try them.

Updated October 23, 2026

1. Shorten the stroke

Most people press the spacebar all the way to the bottom and lift their thumb high between presses. Neither is needed. A key registers part of the way down, so every millimetre beyond that is wasted time. Rest your thumb lightly on the key and practise tiny, quick taps that only just make it click. Your speed will often jump by one or two CPS from this change alone.

2. Fix your hand position

  • Rest your wrist or the heel of your hand on the desk or the laptop's palm rest. A floating hand moves more and tires faster.
  • Use the side of your thumb near the tip, not the pad. The side is firmer and bounces off the key more quickly.
  • Keep your thumb slightly bent and relaxed. A locked, straight thumb is slower and cramps sooner.
  • Press near the middle of the spacebar. The ends of a long key can feel heavier and less even.

3. Use two thumbs

A single thumb has to press and lift before it can press again. Two thumbs working in turn can overlap those movements, so while one is lifting the other is already coming down. It feels clumsy at first. Start slowly, aiming for an even left-right rhythm, and only then speed up. Within a few sessions most people pass their single-thumb record.

4. Find a rhythm

Random bursts feel fast but rarely are. A steady rhythm is easier to keep up and easier to speed up gradually. Some players count in their head or tap along to a fast song. After each run, check the consistency score and the per-second chart on the Spacebar Clicker: even bars mean your rhythm held.

5. Pace the long tests

In 30, 60 and 100 second tests, a flat-out start almost always ends in a slump. Open at about 90 percent of your top speed and hold it. If you still have energy in the last ten seconds, push then. Compare two runs on the chart, one with a sprint start and one paced, and you will usually see the paced run finish higher.

A seven-day practice plan

Ten minutes a day is enough. Rest a minute between runs, and stop early if your hand feels sore.

When to stop

Fast repeated pressing is a strain on small muscles and tendons. Ache that fades after a short rest is normal. Sharp pain, numbness, tingling or soreness that lasts into the next day is not, so stop and give your hand a few days off. If it continues, talk to a doctor or physiotherapist.
